# KNOWLEDGE.md

Re-read before every decision. Operational rules, not summaries.

## 1. How indie software gets to $1K, $5K, $10K MRR

- **The median outcome is failure.** Most indie apps never pass $1K/month. RevenueCat's data across tens of thousands of subscription apps: roughly 4 in 5 never cross $1K MRR, and the top 5% take almost all the revenue. Assume the base rate applies to you.
- **$0 to $1K takes months, not weeks, and is almost entirely founder-driven distribution.** Nearly every documented first $1K came from the founder personally talking to people: community posts, cold DMs, replying to complaints, doing the work by hand first. Not from launching and waiting.
- **$1K to $5K is usually one channel that repeats.** The businesses that got there found one source of customers that kept producing (a subreddit, an app store keyword, a marketplace listing, a partner) and worked it for a year. Founders who kept switching channels stalled.
- **$5K to $10K is retention, not acquisition.** Churn above ~5%/month caps MRR no matter how many signups arrive. At 5% monthly churn with 20 new customers a month at $25, revenue plateaus around $10K. The jump comes from lower churn (a daily-use or workflow-embedded product) or higher price, not more marketing.
- **Timelines from public revenue logs (Indie Hackers, MicroConf, X build-in-public):** first paying customer 1–3 months after starting outreach; $1K MRR at 6–12 months; $5K at 18–30 months. Faster cases nearly always had an existing audience or a market they already worked in.
- **What failed:** consumer apps with no distribution ("build it and post on Product Hunt"); markets where the customer is broke (students, hobbyists at low intensity); products whose value is invisible until months of use; anything needing paid ads before revenue; founders who built for 6 months before one conversation.
- **Common shape of the winners:** B2B or prosumer, one narrow customer with a recurring painful job, $15–$50/month, founder reachable to customers, boring problem, no venture ambitions.

## 2. Why apps fail

- **No pull.** They solve a problem nobody is already trying to solve with money or time. Test: is the customer currently paying for anything, or hacking a spreadsheet, to do this? If not, there is no market, only an idea.
- **The customer can't be found.** "Small businesses" and "students" are not customers; they are populations. A customer is someone you can list by name, find in a specific place, and message today.
- **Retention is invisible until it kills you.** Signups feel like progress. Only week-4 retention and paid renewal count.
- **Pricing too low to survive.** $5/month products need thousands of customers. At solo scale, price so that 100 customers is a real business.
- **The founder is the only user.** Building for yourself is fine only when you are a typical member of a reachable market.

## 3. Pricing psychology

- **Hard paywall beats freemium for solo founders.** Free users cost support and produce almost no conversion (typically 1–3%). A free trial (7–14 days, card-optional) or a generous but time-boxed trial converts better and filters for people with the problem. Use freemium only when free users are themselves the distribution (viral or network products).
- **Annual plans are a churn tool, not a discount.** Offer annual at ~2 months free. Annual buyers churn far less and pay upfront cash. Default the pricing page to annual.
- **Charge for the outcome, not the feature list.** People pay for "my students actually practise" not "assignment tracking."
- **Anchor with a higher tier.** Two or three tiers, the middle one is the real product. A single price with no anchor reads as arbitrary.
- **Raise prices early.** Early customers are the least price-sensitive because they have the worst pain. Grandfather them; new customers pay more.
- **Price per seat only if seats map to value.** Per-coach or per-business flat pricing is simpler to sell to solo operators.

## 4. Distribution at zero budget

- **Go where the complaint already lives.** Subreddits, Facebook groups, Discords, niche forums, review sites for competitors. Search for the exact words of the problem, reply helpfully, mention the product only when it answers the question.
- **Outbound works if it's specific.** A DM that names the person's situation ("saw your post about students not doing their homework") gets replies. Generic pitches get ignored. Volume of 5–10 genuinely personal messages a day beats 100 templated ones.
- **Do it by hand first.** Offer to do the job manually for the first 5 people. It validates, produces testimonials, and teaches what the software must do.
- **Communities have rules.** Read them. Most allow a "I built this for X, feedback wanted" post once. Most ban repeated promotion. The reputation comes from answering other people's questions for weeks first.
- **Word of mouth needs a reason.** Products get referred when they make the user look good to someone else (a coach's report to parents, a shareable output). Build the shareable artefact in from day one.
- **Directories and marketplaces are compounding but slow.** Listings on tool directories, "alternatives to X" pages, and app marketplaces produce a trickle that grows. Set them up early, expect nothing for months.
- **Paid ads before $5K MRR are a way to lose money faster.** Skip.

## 5. Operational rules from the canon

**The Mom Test (Fitzpatrick)**
- Never ask if the idea is good. Ask about their life: what they did last time the problem came up, what it cost them, what they tried.
- Talk about specifics in the past, not hypotheticals. "Would you use..." is worthless. "When did this last happen?" is data.
- Compliments are noise. Commitment is signal: time, reputation, or money. An email address is a weak commitment; a paid pre-order is strong.
- If they don't currently do anything about the problem, it's not a problem they'll pay to solve.
- Learn something that could make you kill the idea in every conversation, or you weren't listening.

**The Lean Startup (Ries)**
- Build the smallest thing that tests the riskiest assumption, measure real behaviour, then decide. The riskiest assumption is almost always "will anyone pay," not "can I build it."
- Vanity metrics (signups, page views) lie. Use cohorts: of the people who started in week N, how many are still here and paying?
- Pivot when the data says so, not when you're bored.

**Zero to One (Thiel)**
- Start with a tiny market you can dominate, then expand. Being the obvious tool for chess coaches beats being the tenth tool for "educators."
- Ask what you know that others don't. A real edge is a market insight, not a feature.
- Distribution is a product feature. A product without a distribution plan is incomplete.

**Traction (Weinberg & Mares)**
- Spend 50% of effort on distribution from day one, not after launch.
- Bullseye: list all channels, test three cheaply, double down on the one that works.
- Founders overestimate their favourite channel and underestimate unglamorous ones (SEO, directories, partnerships).

**Obviously Awesome (Dunford)**
- Positioning is choosing the competitive alternative you're compared against. Pick one where you obviously win.
- The alternative is often "spreadsheet and WhatsApp," not another SaaS.
- Name the category in the customer's words. Lead with the one thing they can't get elsewhere.

**$100M Offers (Hormozi)**
- Value = (dream outcome × likelihood) ÷ (time delay × effort). Raise the top, cut the bottom.
- Stack the offer: the core plus the things that remove the customer's objections (setup done for them, templates, a guarantee).
- Guarantees convert. "If your students don't practise more in 30 days, don't pay."
- Sell to someone with purchasing power, pain, and reachability. Two out of three is not enough.

## 6. Decision rules for this project

1. The customer must be nameable, findable in a specific online place today, and already spending time or money on the problem.
2. The product must be usable and valuable within the first session, and produce something the customer shows to someone else.
3. Recurring because the job recurs weekly, not because a subscription was bolted on.
4. Nothing core may depend on an 18+ account. Payments through an invoice and bank transfer are acceptable for the first customers; a parent-held merchant-of-record account is the later path.
5. First 10 customers via direct, personal outreach in the communities where the complaint already lives. If that plan can't be written concretely, the market fails.
6. Kill criteria: fewer than 10 real complaints found, no current spend, or no concrete outreach list.
